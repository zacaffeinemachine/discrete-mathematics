A row of $n$ distinct numbers is written on a board. A move picks two entries standing next
to each other whose order is wrong, meaning that the left one is larger than the right one,
and swaps them. Nobody tells us which such pair to pick, and at a given moment there may be
several.

> Show that the swapping stops after finitely many moves, whatever the choices are, and
> that the row is in increasing order when no move is left.

Here nothing shrinks. The row keeps its $n$ entries from beginning to end, so there is no
smaller version of the problem to point at. Something else has to do the descending.

## What to notice

Call a pair of positions $i < j$ an **inversion** when $a_i > a_j$ — the two entries are in
the wrong order, whether or not they are neighbours. The arcs above the row are exactly
these pairs, and $I$ counts them.

Now make a move and watch $I$. It goes down by one. Not by two, not sometimes by none: by
exactly one, every time, because swapping two neighbours changes the standing of that one
pair and of no other. A count of arcs cannot fall by one for ever.

Then run the same row twice, choosing differently each time. Both runs take $I_0$ moves.
The choices never mattered.

The other two rules are worth a minute each. Allow *any* out-of-order pair to swap and $I$
still falls, but it can fall by more than one, so two runs need not agree. Allow neighbours
to swap whether or not they are out of order and $I$ can climb — press **Run at random**
and leave it going. It has finitely many rows to visit and it still never settles, which is
worth remembering the next time an argument claims that finitely many states force a stop.
